## Research and ranking

Collect relevant language with source IDs, short quotations or faithful paraphrases, audience context, and the concern it expresses. Keep observations separate from hypotheses. Group synonymous concerns without erasing useful customer wording. Do not count the same review republished across sites multiple times or equate marketer copy with customer testimony.

Within each category, summarize up to three recurring patterns in the frequency columns. If multiple subtopics need the five available rows, group each row coherently and retain its topic and sources in the evidence ledger. Do not fill every blank for appearance. If sources do not support an ordering, mark the rank unverified and list candidate themes as hypotheses separately.

Report the observed sample and counting unit, such as “six distinct reviewers across three sources.” Counts describe that sample only. Separate frequency from importance: an uncommon objection may still prevent purchase.

For the 1–10 importance score, state the unit being scored (category or a defined row's topic) and its reasoning. A practical judgment rubric is 1–3 for peripheral concerns, 4–6 for meaningful preferences, 7–8 for strong decision factors, and 9–10 for a central desired outcome or evidenced purchase blocker. This rubric is an adaptation. Use unavailable when evidence does not support a judgment; never imply a measured statistical or performance score.

Keep the evidence ledger alongside a text grid or in an accompanying research note when filling the original workbook. This preserves the template while allowing reviewers to trace sources, counts, ranks, and scoring rationale.

## Use the glossary in copy

For each term, give its audience meaning and useful notes: exact source, spelling or phrasing, intent context, whether buyers actually use it, and any ambiguity. Preserve exact language for research but adapt respectfully for advertising. Do not force insider jargon into an ad aimed at novices.

Translate the grid into candidate angles:

- Hopes & Dreams → desired outcome or specific benefit.
- Pains & Fears → a problem the offer credibly helps resolve, preserving the evidenced stakes and the buyer's private question without inventing a threat.
- Barriers & Uncertainties → a decision question, proof requirement, or objection to answer.
- Glossary → natural vocabulary that makes the ad sound relevant to this buyer.

For each selected insight, record its source language, triggering scene, stakes or desired relief, private question, ad angle, supporting page section, and exact headline/description candidate. Label creative interpretations; make the insight visible in the selected copy. A research finding cannot establish a product claim by itself: the advertiser's evidence and destination must support the promise.
