# Deep Halo research protocol

Read for a new Halo scan. For a copy revision, inspect the existing research
against this gate and fill material gaps rather than restarting blindly.
This six-cycle method is an adaptation of the Halo principle, not a procedure
attributed verbatim to Sabri Suby or the original worksheet.

## Source selection

Start with a source map: buyer role, buying situation, offer, geography,
language, relevant date range, known competitors/alternatives, available
customer evidence and gaps. Select source families because they can answer
a research question, not because they are easy to scrape:

- Authorized first-party conversations: discovery and sales calls, win/loss
  interviews, objections in CRM notes and customer interviews.
- Experience after purchase: support tickets, cancellations, onboarding
  friction, refunds and implementation retrospectives.
- Independent reviews: detailed positive, mixed and negative accounts of the
  offer category or alternatives. Distinguish verified experience from hearsay.
- Peer discussions: practitioner forums, professional communities, relevant
  Reddit threads, public Q&A and substantive comment discussions.
- Workarounds and switching: migration accounts, buyer-written comparisons,
  postmortems and explanations of why someone abandoned an alternative.

Vendor blogs and competitor landing pages help explain positioning and offers;
they are not first-person buyer evidence. Expert opinions, adjacent personas
and old accounts may suggest a lead to investigate, but label their distance
from the actual buyer. Ten posts syndicated from one account are one account.
Multiple platforms hosting the same type of review are not four distinct
source families. Obtain permission for access or outreach where needed; no
scan implies permission to join private groups or contact people.

## Initial scan plus five additional cycles

Each cycle records its question, queries or supplied sources inspected, new
account IDs, findings, contradictions, remaining gaps and next research move.
Follow links and replies into the original context. A zero-result search can
be a documented attempt, not a completed finding. Do not call repeated reads
of the same evidence five additional discovery cycles.

| Cycle | Investigate | Evidence that advances the scan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0: Buying situation | What happened immediately before the search? What role owns the decision and which alternatives are plausible? | Accounts with an identifiable trigger, decision and relevant buyer context. |
| 1: Lived scene | Where does the problem intrude? What gets redone, delayed, checked at night or brought into a difficult meeting? | A concrete incident, workaround, conversation or sequence of work, in buyer language. Follow the most promising accounts and seek independent examples. |
| 2: Feared consequence | What happens if this stays unresolved? Who notices, who bears the cost, what do they fear having to explain? | Explicit stakes such as money, trust, control or career exposure. If a deeper consequence is only inferred, label it and look for confirmation. |
| 3: Desired future | What would a good solution let them stop doing, protect, accomplish or feel proud of? How would they know? | Specific scenes of relief and ambition, including positive buyer accounts. A meaningful hope can be stronger than a threat. |
| 4: Failed alternatives | What did the last provider promise? What actually happened? What hidden work, surprise cost or dismissive response caused resentment or switching? | Named alternative where evidenced, complaint, consequence, workaround and proof the buyer now demands. Include doing nothing and doing it internally as alternatives. |
| 5: Challenge and synthesis | Who disagrees? When did the alternative work? Does the intense account fit this persona and search intent? | Independent corroboration, counterexamples, persona boundaries, gaps and final angle candidates with confidence labels. |

Use buyers' own phrases as the next queries: a specific failure plus role,
category, switching language or desired outcome. Explore replies where people
explain why the obvious advice did not work. Pair strong negative accounts
with successful ones so the result does not become a collection of grievances.
Do not search only for words such as terrified or fired and mistake that
selection for the audience's dominant psychology.

## Evidence ledger

For each useful account retain:

- Account/source ID, stable URL or authorized internal source locator, source
  family, source date when known, inspection date and accessible context.
- Buyer role and situation; direct experience vs secondhand account; relevance
  to the campaign and deduplication key.
- Short exact excerpt within quotation limits, or a labeled faithful
  paraphrase. Preserve distinctive verbiage without copying whole threads.
- Trigger → immediate problem → consequence → desired relief → failed
  alternative → question or proof needed. Leave unobserved fields unknown.
- Explicit emotion versus inferred emotion; intensity and decision relevance
  as explained editorial judgments; corroborating and contradicting IDs.

Maintain a competitor-complaint view: alternative, buyer complaint, observed
consequence, source/date, buyer's new proof requirement, and potential angle.
An individual complaint does not establish a universal defect or authorize
an accusation in an ad. Often the useful ad addresses the failure mechanism
without naming a competitor at all.

Keep the most useful language, including frustration and embarrassment, in
the research. Do not translate it into corporate summaries prematurely. The
public copy should earn recognition, not shame the buyer or insult others.

## Coverage and completion

For a full live scan, aim for 20 or more distinct, relevant first-person
accounts across at least four source families. Record actual counts and
the inclusion rule. More accounts are not automatically better; relevance,
depth and independent corroboration matter. This target is a default depth
check, not a claim of statistical significance or a reason to invent evidence.

The deep scan is complete only when:

1. All six cycles have a research log with inspected evidence or explicit
   access/coverage gaps; all five follow-ups have been attempted as distinct
   investigations within the permitted sources.
2. Hopes, fears, practical problems, barriers and failed-alternative complaints
   are represented, or an absence is stated rather than filled with invention.
3. Each lead insight contains a recognizable scene, meaningful stakes or
   relief, a private question and an earned answer. It has at least two
   independent relevant accounts, or is explicitly a single-account test
   hypothesis rather than an established recurring theme.
4. Counterevidence and sampling bias have been examined, and the selected
   insight still fits this buyer and the actual search decision.
5. The grid, glossary, competitor-complaint view and insight-to-angle brief
   are traceable to the ledger. When copy is authorized, add the exact
   insight-to-ad bridges; research-only work does not draft ads. The writer
   can explain what changed after the initial scan and why each selected
   angle deserves to lead.

If sources remain thin, mark the research **partial** and name exactly what
would resolve the gap. Do not loop indefinitely to manufacture volume: after
the six bounded cycles, report the shortfall and ask for the next source.
In source-limited/offline work, a six-lens analysis of supplied material is
useful, but it is not a completed live scan with five rounds of new discovery.
Copy can be delivered provisionally when requested; it must not be labeled
fully researched. If the user requested research review first, stop at that
reviewable research deliverable.

## When the user can help

Ask during research when the evidence gap becomes clear, not after generic
copy has been written. Make the request concrete and explain its value:

> Public reviews explain implementation delays, but not what those delays
> cost the buyer personally. Do you have recent lost-deal notes, cancellation
> reasons or recorded sales calls where customers explain why this matters?
> A few relevant examples would help test the accountability angle.

Other useful requests: buyer community names, competitor shortlist, repeated
sales objections, support escalations, customer interview access, or what the
sales team hears after an initial polite answer. Invite the user to brainstorm
source locations. Continue research that does not depend on the answer.

## Calibration examples — fictional research, not market facts

Assume a fictional bookkeeping packet explicitly reports that owners spend
Sunday rebuilding cash balances, dread a Friday payroll decision, and want to
know what cash is available. The destination explains reconciliation and the
monthly reporting process; it offers no emergency payroll service.

| Generic candidate | Research-led candidate | Why / boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Professional Bookkeeping | Still Doing Books on Sunday? | Recognizable lost-time scene; pair with what the monthly service actually does. |
| Accurate Financial Insights | Afraid to Open Your Books? | Names evidenced avoidance; use only if the packet actually supports that fear. |
| Trusted Accounting Partner | What Can You Actually Spend? | Sells an answer to a cash-visibility question; the page must explain the relevant information and its limits. |

"Payroll Friday? Fixed Today" would invent both a service and a timing promise.
"Know What's in Your Package" may be useful support copy when scope is the
objection, but it cannot stand in for a deeper, better-supported lead insight.
The exact headline must be counted and its combinations checked before use.
